Landslide mitigation: slope stability, avoided deaths, and their value

Purpose

Value the deaths that forest root cohesion averts by holding hillslopes together. The service is computed as a difference between two runs of the same model on the same grid: one in which forest roots reinforce the soil as the land-cover map says they do, and one in which root cohesion is removed entirely. Everything else – terrain, soil, rainfall, population, roads, and the fitted statistical models – is identical between the two, so the difference in predicted deaths is attributable to root reinforcement alone.

This note describes what the code in global_invest/landslide_mitigation/ does. The science is a fold of m-braaksma/landslide_mitigation v0.2.0 (folded 2026-08-16); the arithmetic lives in landslide_mitigation_chain.py as pure functions, the raster drivers in landslide_mitigation_functions.py, and the pipeline in landslide_mitigation_tasks.py.

Grid and inputs

Everything is computed on the NSIDC EASE-Grid 2.0 global 1km grid (EPSG:6933), built from the authoritative EASE2_M01km.gpd grid definition rather than assumed. EASE-Grid is equal-area, so a pixel count converts to real area by one constant multiply and no latitude weighting is needed anywhere in the calculation. Every input is warped onto that exact grid and then checked against it for matching size, pixel resolution and projection before the model runs.

Input Source Resampling onto the 1km grid
Elevation SEALS alt_m.tif (shared base data) average
Agro-ecological zones FAO GAEZ v5 AEZ57 mode (categorical)
Forest cover ESA-CCI annual land cover class weights, then average
Landslide events UGLC point records coordinate transform, not a warp
Population LandScan, per year sum (conserves counts)
Soil texture, organic carbon, bulk density SoilGrids 0-5, 5-15, 15-30cm thickness-weighted mean, then average
Saturated conductivity HiHydroSoil 0-5, 5-15, 15-30cm thickness-weighted mean, then average
Soil and sediment thickness Pelletier/ORNL DAAC average
Mean annual precipitation WorldClim BIO12 (1970-2000 normal) average
Annual maximum daily rainfall ERA5-Land, per year bilinear (upsampling)
Road density GRIP4 total density, m/km² bilinear (upsampling)

The three soil-depth intervals are combined by their own thickness (5, 10 and 15cm), and only where each interval has data, so a pixel missing the deepest layer is the mean of the two shallower ones rather than a hole.

Forest cover enters as a root-reinforcement weight, not as a binary forest mask. Closed-canopy and flooded tree-cover classes take weight 1; the two mosaic tree/shrub-herbaceous classes are 50/50 land cover and take 0.5; sparse tree cover below 15% takes 0, as being below meaningful root reinforcement. Every other class is 0. Averaging that 0-1 weight field onto the 1km grid gives each pixel a fractional forest share.

Slope

Slope is computed with gdal.DEMProcessing (Horn 1981) from the raw elevation at four times the target resolution (~250m) and then averaged down to 1km. Computing slope at 1km directly would flatten exactly the terrain the model is about. Because the grid is already in metres, no degree-to-metre scale factor enters.

The elevation used for the hydrology is separately pit-filled at 1km: the trusted 300m DEM is already pit-filled, but averaging it down to 1km can create new local minima. D8 flow direction and flow accumulation run on the pit-filled version, and the accumulated pixel count is scaled to an upslope contributing area in m².

Slope stability with and without forest roots

The stability model is the infinite-slope form used across the SHALSTAB / SINMAP / TRIGRS family. For every pixel,

\[ SI \;=\; \frac{\tan\phi}{\tan\beta} \;+\; \frac{c_{\text{soil}} + c_{\text{root}}}{\gamma\,h\,\sin\beta\,\cos\beta} \;-\; \min\!\left(\frac{q}{T\,\sin\beta},\,1\right) \]

where \(\phi\) is the internal friction angle, \(\beta\) the slope, \(c_{\text{soil}}\) the soil’s own cohesion, \(c_{\text{root}}\) the root reinforcement, \(\gamma\) the soil unit weight, \(h\) the soil depth, \(q\) the specific discharge and \(T\) the transmissivity. The first term is friction against geometry, the second is cohesion holding the slope together, and the third is the destabilising effect of water. Higher \(SI\) is more stable.

The four soil terms are derived from the mapped soil properties:

  • Friction angle \(\phi = 25 + (\text{sand}\% - \text{clay}\%)/20\), clipped to 15-40 degrees: sandier soils resist shear at a steeper angle than clay-rich ones.
  • Soil cohesion \(c_{\text{soil}} = 2 + 0.03\,\text{clay}\% + 0.1\,\text{organic carbon}\%\) kPa, clipped to 0-50 kPa.
  • Unit weight \(\gamma = \rho\,g\), converting SoilGrids bulk density in Mg/m³ to kN/m³.
  • Transmissivity \(T = K_{\text{sat}} \times h\), with HiHydroSoil’s integer conductivity unscaled and converted to m/day first.

The specific discharge is static rather than event-based: the WorldClim annual rainfall total is spread evenly over the year, applied to the pixel’s upslope contributing area, and divided by the cell width it enters through. The hydrological term is physically a saturation ratio, so it is capped at 1; uncapped, the heavily right-skewed upslope area drives most pixels onto the stability floor.

Root cohesion is the only thing that differs between the two runs. It is \(c_{\text{root}} = \text{forest share} \times c_{\text{root,max}}\) with \(c_{\text{root,max}} = 5\) kPa in the observed run and \(0\) in the full_impacts counterfactual.

Three exclusions and clips are part of the method rather than defensive coding, and each changes results:

  • Pixels flatter than 2 degrees are excluded entirely. Infinite-slope theory does not apply there, and without the exclusion both the friction and hydrological terms blow up near \(\beta = 0\) and swamp the forest-cover signal – the median observed-versus-counterfactual difference was exactly zero before it was added.
  • Slope is clipped to 0.5-89.5 degrees and the friction angle to 15-40 degrees before the trigonometry, since \(\tan\beta\) goes to zero at flat and diverges near vertical.
  • \(SI\) itself is clipped to \([-10, 10]\).

The observed index is computed for every modelling year and the prediction year; the counterfactual only for the prediction year, since a counterfactual has no meaning during calibration.

Calibrating stability to landslide probability

Recorded landslides are rare relative to land pixels, so the hazard model is fitted on a stratified case-control sample rather than on the whole grid.

Cases are UGLC events. The events are filtered to those with a usable location accuracy – the recorded accuracy must be present, positive, not one of the fill codes UGLC uses, and no coarser than 1000m – and then reprojected to EASE-Grid metres. Several events can fall in one 1km pixel, so within a year they are collapsed to one row per pixel carrying the highest fatality count of the group: the model is fitted on pixel-years.

Controls are pixel-years drawn uniformly at random over the grid and kept only where the GAEZ raster says there is land, at 25 controls per case, with a fixed seed so the draw is reproducible. Each row, case or control, is then sampled for its stability index, annual maximum daily rainfall, population, slope, road density and GAEZ zone. Rows missing stability, rainfall or zone are dropped – most of them are the near-flat pixels the stability model excluded.

An ordinary logit of case status on stability index and extreme rainfall is fitted on the modelling years. Its slope coefficients are consistent estimates of the population slopes, but its intercept carries the oversampling of cases, so it is corrected (Prentice and Pyke 1979):

\[ \alpha_{\text{corrected}} \;=\; \alpha_{\text{raw}} \;-\; \log\!\frac{\tau/(1-\tau)}{\pi/(1-\pi)} \]

\(\tau\) is the case fraction in the sample. \(\pi\) is the prevalence in the population, and it is not assumed: it is counted from the UGLC annual panels themselves, as event pixel-years over SI-eligible land pixel-years across the same years used for training. Restricting the denominator to the pixels the stability model actually scores keeps the prevalence on the same population the model predicts over. The per-year counts behind it are exported as an audit table.

To build those panels, each UGLC event is stamped onto the grid as a disc of its own location accuracy rather than as a single pixel: the binary panel marks every pixel the disc covers, and the mortality panel spreads the event’s fatalities over the disc with a linear distance decay \(1 - d/r\), summed across events.

The fit is scored by AUC on the training years and on the held-out prediction year.

Fatalities given a landslide

Severity is a two-part hurdle model, fitted only on realized landslides (the cases) in the modelling years, on the same four covariates in both parts: log(1+population), extreme rainfall, slope and road density.

  • Part A, a logit, gives \(\Pr(\text{fatalities} > 0 \mid \text{landslide})\). If the maximum likelihood fit fails on separation, it falls back to an L2-regularized fit; the coefficients are then shrunk toward zero and the standard errors are not comparable to an unpenalized fit, so that fallback is a point estimate rather than inference.
  • Part B, an OLS on the fatal subsample, gives \(E[\log(\text{fatalities}) \mid \text{fatal}]\).

Back-transforming part B needs care: \(\exp\) of a predicted log understates the expectation by Jensen’s inequality. The correction is Duan’s (1983) smearing factor, the mean of \(\exp\) of the part-B residuals, which estimates the bias from the residuals themselves rather than assuming log-normal errors. Expected fatalities given a landslide are therefore

\[ E[\text{deaths} \mid \text{landslide}] \;=\; \Pr(\text{fatal}) \times \exp\!\big(X\beta_B\big) \times \text{smearing}. \]

A separate diagnostic refits both parts with the stability index in place of slope, to check whether stability subsumes slope’s information in severity. It is written to its own output and never overwrites the production coefficients.

Prediction and avoided mortality

Prediction runs tile by tile over the land-containing tiles of the grid (2000 pixels per side by default), so the global rasters are assembled from independent pieces.

For each scenario, expected deaths per pixel-year are

\[ \text{deaths} \;=\; \Pr(\text{landslide} \mid SI, \text{rain}) \times E[\text{deaths} \mid \text{landslide}]. \]

Rainfall is the prediction year’s, and the severity covariates do not vary by scenario, so the severity expectation is computed once per tile and reused. The scenarios differ only through the stability index that enters the hazard probability – which is to say, only through root cohesion.

Avoided mortality is then the counterfactual minus the observed:

\[ \text{avoided deaths} \;=\; \text{deaths}_{\text{full\_impacts}} \;-\; \text{deaths}_{\text{observed}}. \]

Physically this cannot be negative – removing root cohesion cannot make a slope safer – so pixels where it comes out negative are counted and warned about rather than silently floored.

Valuation

Avoided deaths are priced at the value of a statistical life, per country, in 2019 constant USD to match the rest of GEP.

Countries with a published OECD country estimate take it directly. The published figures are 2022 USD and are reported with a power-of-ten multiplier, so they are scaled up and then deflated to 2019 using the PMPRB CPI-based cumulative price-adjustment factor of 1.050 from 2019 to 2022.

Countries without a direct estimate take their income group’s figure from Table 6.1 of OECD (2025), Mortality Risk Valuation in Policy Assessment: 7.1 USD million for high-income OECD, 7.9 for high-income non-OECD, and 1.1 for the upper-middle, lower-middle and low-income groups. A row whose income group is unclassified takes the global figure of 2.7 USD million. These are 2022 USD millions and are deflated the same way.

The per-country values are joined onto the EE r264 correspondence and rasterized onto the 1km grid, so every pixel carries its country’s VSL. The service’s value raster is the avoided-death raster times the VSL raster, and country totals are the zonal sums of both rasters over the correspondence polygons.

Note that the r264 correspondence splits large countries into sub-regions. Each sub-region carries its country’s ISO3 and therefore its national VSL, which is what the rasterization needs; a one-row-per-country table has to sum the sub-regions back together, which is what the results report does.

What the module does not do

  • The stability model is static: one specific discharge from a climatological rainfall normal, not an event-based or seasonal hydrology.
  • Rainfall enters the hazard model as the annual maximum daily total, so hazard is a per-pixel-year probability, not a per-storm one.
  • Transmissivity units are flagged as unverified in the code and have not been checked against the HiHydroSoil documentation here.
  • The module has been run end to end once on the cluster, but its numbers have not been replicated against a machine-readable v0.2.0 output from the author, so they remain provisional.
